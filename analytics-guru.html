A Project C field guide

Build your analytics guru

15 things worth checking if you run a newsletter, a paid membership, or both. Most of them are invisible until someone goes looking, and several of them can't be fixed retroactively at all.

15 checks 5 phases By Liz Kelly Nelson August 2026
Where this came from

I spent a day auditing every system Project C runs on: the newsletter, the website, the payments, the community database, the social accounts. I expected to tidy up some dashboards.

None of what I found was carelessness. It's what happens when you wire together six services that each work fine on their own. Nobody owns the seams between them. This is the checklist I wish I'd had.

What turned up instead

01

Membership records in four different systems that quietly disagreed with each other about who was a member.

02

A bank statement descriptor still naming a platform we left more than a year ago. Every member saw the old name.

03

Search data that didn't exist, because nobody had ever turned the reporting on. That history is gone for good.

01

Start the clock

Do these first, today, even if you do nothing else on this list. Analytics tools do not reconstruct the past. They start recording the day you switch them on. Every week you wait is a week of history that never existed.

01

Verify your site in Google Search Console

It's free, it takes about ten minutes, and it's the only way to see what people actually type into a search box to find you.

What happened here

We had real, steady organic search traffic arriving every month and zero visibility into a single query behind it. Search Console had never been set up. That history is simply gone.

How →

Add a Domain property, not a URL prefix, so it covers every subdomain including your newsletter. You'll add one DNS TXT record to verify. Then add a URL-prefix property too. It auto-verifies, and some integrations will only accept that kind.

02

Put analytics on your website

Any of them. A privacy-friendly one avoids the cookie-banner problem entirely.

The trap

A freshly installed tool shows every metric as "+100%" because the previous period is empty. That looks like explosive growth and means nothing. Note your install date somewhere you'll find it again, and don't quote growth figures until you've cleared it.

How →

One script tag in your site's head. If you're on a site builder, there's a settings field for it.

03

Write down which sources have history and which don't

Not every number you have goes back the same distance, and mixing them quietly produces nonsense.

Why it matters

Payment records may go back years. Newsletter platforms usually keep everything. But social platforms only show rolling 30-to-90-day windows, and you cannot retrieve last spring. If you want that history, you have to save it yourself, on a schedule.

04

Route a weekly report into your email

Most analytics tools will email you a summary. Turn it on and send it to the address you actually read.

The real reason

A dashboard requires you to remember it exists, be logged in, and be at the right computer. An email arrives whether or not you're paying attention, and it stays searchable forever. That makes it the cheapest permanent archive you will ever build.

02

Follow the money

If you take recurring payments, this is where the recoverable money usually is. Not in growth tactics. In subscriptions that lapse for technical reasons rather than deliberate ones, with nothing set up to flag it.

05

Audit your failed payments

Filter your payment processor for failed charges over the last few months, then group them by customer.

Why this one first

Cards expire. Banks block recurring charges. Processors retry a few times and then close the subscription automatically. None of that generates an alert. It is the cheapest revenue you will ever recover, because nobody involved decided to leave, and it stays completely invisible unless someone thinks to run the query.

How →

A dozen failed charges is usually only a few people being retried over and over. Count distinct customers, not attempts.

06

Learn the difference between "past due" and "canceled"

They look identical from the outside and they need completely different emails.

Why it bites

A past-due member still has a live subscription. Send them a billing-portal link and updating their card fixes everything. A canceled member has nothing to update, and that same link is a dead end. Sending the wrong one makes you look disorganized at the exact moment you're asking for money.

07

Read your own bank statement descriptor

Find the field that controls what appears on your members' card statements, and check that it says what you think it says.

What happened here

Ours still named a platform we had migrated away from more than a year earlier. Every member saw the old name on their statement. It's set once when you open the account and nothing ever prompts you to revisit it, which is exactly why nobody does. Unrecognized descriptors are a leading cause of chargebacks.

08

Confirm that people who signed up were actually charged

Look for subscriptions that were created but never completed their first payment.

The failure mode

A subscription can be created, fire its whole welcome sequence and grant access while its first invoice is never attempted. Not declined. Simply never charged, then quietly voided a day later. Every system downstream behaves as though payment succeeded, because from where they sit it did. It's rare, and precisely because it's rare nobody thinks to build a check for it.

How →

In Stripe, look for subscription status incomplete_expired. Check it once a month. You're looking for a rare event with a permanent cost.

03

Check the plumbing

Nobody builds a broken system on purpose. You build one service at a time, each one sensible on its own, and the gaps appear in the joins between them, where no single tool is responsible for noticing.

09

List every place that holds a copy of "who is a member"

Write them down. Payment processor, member database, community platform, email list, course tool.

Why it matters

Here it was four separate systems, each holding a partial and slightly different answer. Nobody had ever asked them the same question on the same day. When we finally did, they disagreed.

10

Find out whether those systems talk back

Open your payment processor's webhook settings and look at the list.

What to look for

An empty list means data is only moving one way. Your signup form creates the subscription and nothing ever reports back. Billing status changes on one side and the other side never hears about it. That isn't a bug anyone wrote. It's a conversation nobody started.

11

Reconcile the lists on a schedule

Until the systems talk to each other, do it by hand or on a timer. Who's marked active but isn't paying, and who's paying but isn't marked active.

Be honest about scale

At a small membership, a weekly reconciliation catches almost everything a real-time integration would, for none of the engineering. Build the automation when the volume earns it, not because it sounds more professional.

04

Who's finding you, and who's just taking

Two questions most independent publishers have never asked about their own site. Both of them matter more now than they did two years ago.

12

Separate reach from traffic from revenue

Line up, for each platform: audience size, impressions, and the visitors it actually sent to your site.

The surprise here

The platform generating vastly more impressions sent almost nobody to the website. A quieter platform with a smaller audience sent eight times more traffic. Both numbers are real. They're just answering different questions, and only one of them feeds the thing you own.

How →

Don't conclude the loud platform is worthless. It may be doing genuine discovery work. Test it instead. One month, one explicit call to action, then look again.

13

Look at which bots are crawling your work

Your host or newsletter platform may report this. Otherwise your server logs will.

What we found

Crawler traffic was a meaningful share of all requests, none of it blocked, and AI training crawlers slightly outnumbered search-engine crawlers. The single heaviest crawler wasn't Google. It was a training crawler that sends no readers and appears in no results page.

14

Make the AI-crawler decision on purpose

Whatever you choose, choose it. Right now most publishers are allowing everything by default, having never looked.

Both cases are real

Training crawlers take your work and give nothing back. But being absent from AI answers removes you from a system that increasingly answers people's questions directly, and for independent journalism, invisibility has its own cost. Blocking search crawlers is a different and much more damaging decision than blocking training crawlers. Don't confuse the two.

How →

A robots.txt rule per crawler. Compliance is voluntary and not all of them honor it, so treat it as a stated preference rather than a lock.

05

Set a rhythm you'll keep

The last one is the one that decides whether any of the other fourteen survive past this week.

15

Check in less often than feels natural

Write down a baseline today. Then pick a cadence and hold it.

The math of being small

When you have a few dozen paying members, one cancellation is a couple of percent of your revenue. One post that lands swings your social numbers entirely. Reviewing weekly means spending most weeks explaining variance that means nothing, and the reliable result of that is you stop reading the reviews at all. A monthly review you actually act on beats four weekly ones you skim.

How →

A quiet weekly check that only speaks up when something crosses a threshold. A real monthly review where decisions get made. A quarterly look at the slow movers like search position and retention.

Do this part today

The one that can't wait

Join the community →

Almost everything on this list can wait until next month at no cost. Two things can't. Search analytics and site analytics don't backfill.

Whatever you do with the rest of it, turn those two on today. Even badly. Even without a plan. In a month you'll have something. In a year you'll have a baseline. If you wait, you'll have neither, and no amount of effort later will get it back.

Written from a real audit of Project C's own systems in August 2026. Figures have been generalized and no individual account is described. The checks are what matter here, not our findings.
